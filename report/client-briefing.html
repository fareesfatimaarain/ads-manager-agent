Figures used throughout — a £50 cost per sale, a £30 daily test budget — are illustrative, chosen to make the arithmetic easy to follow. Your actual targets and limits are set during configuration and can be changed at any time.

01

The idea in one page

Running paid advertising well is mostly a matter of attention. Somebody has to look at what each ad is doing, work out which results mean something and which are noise, move money toward what is working, and stop what isn’t. Done properly this is slow, repetitive, and easy to get wrong when you’re tired or busy.

This system does that work continuously. It writes new ads, puts them into a controlled test, watches how they perform, and then makes one of three calls: put more money behind it, change one thing about it and try again, or stop it. It does this every day, for every ad, without needing to be asked.

The difficult part is not the automation. It is making the automation trustworthy. Most tools in this space fail in one of two directions: they are so cautious that they need constant supervision and save nobody any time, or they act confidently on evidence that doesn’t support the decision — and spend real money doing it.

The system is allowed to be wrong. It is not allowed to be wrong expensively. Every design decision in this document follows from that sentence.

That principle produces some specific behaviour. The system waits longer than feels necessary before judging an ad, because early numbers are misleading in a predictable direction. It holds a much higher bar for spending more than for spending less, because those two mistakes cost very different amounts. It refuses to act at all when its information is incomplete, rather than acting on the part it happens to have. And a set of hard limits sits between every decision and your ad account — limits written into the software itself, not instructions given to an AI and hoped for.

What it handles

  • Writing ad copy and generating imagery
  • Launching structured tests
  • Collecting and interpreting performance
  • Moving budget between ads
  • Stopping ads that don’t work
  • Recording why it did each of these

What stays with you

  • Who you’re targeting
  • Overall budget ceilings
  • Approving increases in spend
  • Brand rules and claims
  • Starting and pausing campaigns
  • Anything the system flags for review
02

The working cycle

The system runs a continuous loop. Each pass through it produces new ads, gathers fresh evidence about existing ones, and makes decisions where the evidence justifies one. The important detail is the gate near the end: no decision reaches your ad account without passing a safety check first, and no action happens until it has been written down.

Why the loop closes

The dotted line matters more than it looks. When an ad is stopped or scaled, the system doesn’t just record the outcome — it records which idea that ad was testing. Over time this accumulates into a picture of what actually persuades your customers, and that picture shapes what gets written next. The system is not only finding better ads, it is narrowing down better angles.

03

Making the creative

New ads are generated from three inputs: who you sell to, what you’re offering, and your brand kit. The brand kit holds your colours, fonts, tone of voice, and — importantly — the words and claims you are not allowed to make.

Brand rules are applied while writing, not after

This is a small distinction with a large effect. Many systems generate freely and then filter the output, which means a banned claim only gets caught if the filter happens to recognise it. Here the constraints are part of the instruction that produces the ad in the first place, so the system is not writing something it then has to be talked out of. Anything that still slips through is caught before launch, but it is a second line of defence rather than the only one.

Every ad carries the idea it is testing

When an ad is created, it is tagged with the angle it represents — the underlying argument it is making. Two ads might both promote the same product while testing completely different propositions: one leads on saving time, another on avoiding a risk, a third on what other people think.

Why this matters commercially

Without the tag, a winning ad tells you only that a particular combination of words and picture worked. With it, you learn that a certain kind of argument works on your audience — which is a far more useful thing to know, because it tells you what to make next rather than what to copy.

Images

Visuals are produced through a separate image service, working from a concept the system has written to match the ad’s angle and your brand palette. Video is supported by the same structure and can be enabled when you want it. The system is deliberately not locked to a single image provider — that part can be swapped without touching anything else, so you are not exposed to one supplier’s pricing or quality drift.

How many at once

Ads are launched in small groups rather than one at a time. A group tests several distinct angles against each other simultaneously, which is both faster and more informative than testing sequentially — the ads compete under the same conditions, on the same days, against the same audience, so differences between them are meaningful rather than a reflection of when they happened to run.

Each ad in a test group gets its own budget allocation rather than sharing one pot. This is a technical necessity, not a preference: when several ads share a budget, the ad platform decides how to divide attention between them, and it does so unevenly. That unevenness would corrupt any comparison drawn afterwards.
04

Testing that actually holds up

Almost every expensive mistake an automated ad system makes comes from acting on evidence that looked convincing and wasn’t.

Problem one: early numbers lie, and always in the same direction

Someone clicks an ad today and buys three days later. The platform credits that sale back to today — but not straight away. For about three days, the figures for any given day keep rising as sales are attributed backwards.

A system judging yesterday’s ads this morning is therefore working from incomplete information — and the gap is not random. Sales are always undercounted, never over, so every ad looks worse than it truly is and good ads get stopped for no reason. This system waits for the figures to settle before treating them as final.

Problem two: small numbers look more meaningful than they are

Suppose one ad has produced three sales and another has produced one. Is the first three times better? Almost certainly not — a gap that size happens constantly by chance alone. But a naive system sees “300% better” and acts.

A formal statistical test runs before any decision, and it asks a second, separate question: not just “is this better than the others?” but “is this genuinely beating your target?” Being the best of four poor ads is not a reason to spend more money.

What was observedNaive readingWhat the system concludes
3 sales vs 1 sale “Three times better” Not enough evidence. A gap this size arises by chance routinely. Keep both running.
1 sale at £10 cost
(target: £50)		 “Five times better than target” A single sale says almost nothing about the true cost. Confidence is low. Do not scale.
40 sales at £29 cost
(target: £50)		 “Beating target” Agreed — and with high confidence the true cost is below target. Eligible to scale.
£110 spent, no sales
(target: £50)		 “Give it more time” Spending more than twice the target with nothing to show is meaningful in itself. Stop it.

Problem three: checking every day finds winners that aren’t there

This one is subtle and catches most systems out. If you test the same question every day for a month, waiting for a result that looks impressive, something eventually will — purely by chance. Checking repeatedly manufactures false winners.

The system counts how many times it has examined each ad and raises the bar accordingly: evidence that convinces on the first look does not convince on the tenth. The same applies across ads, since with enough candidates one will look good by accident.

The practical consequence

Together these corrections make the system act less often, and later, than a simpler tool would. That is the intended trade: each prevents a specific, well-understood way of confidently reaching the wrong conclusion and spending on it.

05

The three decisions

When an ad has accumulated enough evidence, the system reaches one of three conclusions. Anything short of that is simply left alone to keep running.

01
Scale — put more money behind it

Triggered when an ad is beating your target and the system is confident that it is genuinely doing so rather than having a good week. Budget is raised by a capped percentage — a fixed, configurable step, never a jump — and no more than once in any 24-hour period. Raising a budget too fast disturbs the ad platform’s own delivery and often destroys the performance you were trying to buy more of.

02
Adjust — change one thing and try again

Most ads that fail don’t fail completely. The picture works and the words don’t, or the reverse. When an ad is close to your target but not quite there, the system creates a new version with exactly one element changed — the headline or the image, never both — and runs it alongside the original. Changing one thing at a time is what makes the result interpretable: you learn which change mattered. Chains of adjustment are limited in depth so an ad cannot spawn descendants indefinitely.

03
Stop — take it down

Triggered either by spending well past your target with nothing to show, or by performing clearly and significantly worse than the other ads it is being tested against. Because stopping an ad only forgoes a small test budget, while wrongly scaling one spends real money, the evidence required to stop is deliberately lower than the evidence required to scale.

One protection worth knowing about

An ad that is meeting your target is never stopped for being worse than its neighbours. This sounds obvious but is easy to get wrong: if you always remove the weakest ad in a group, the average of what remains rises, which makes the next-weakest ad look bad, and so on. Left unchecked that logic works its way through the whole group and ends up stopping profitable ads for the crime of sharing a test with an exceptional one. The system only stops ads on absolute underperformance — missing your target — not on relative standing alone.

What the system will not do on its own

There is no fourth decision. The system does not change who your ads are shown to, does not create new campaigns, does not alter your offer, and does not touch anything outside the ads it launched itself. Those remain entirely with you.

06

The safety system

Between every decision and your ad account sits a set of hard limits, checked in the software at the moment of action — not written into an instruction and hoped for.

That distinction is the most important thing in this document. A system merely told not to exceed a limit can misread the instruction or reason its way around it. A limit checked in code cannot be argued with: if an action breaches one, it does not happen, and the refusal is recorded with its reason.

The things it may never do

Change who your ads are shown to. Targeting always requires a person, regardless of any setting.
×

×

×

×

07

Who approves what

Not every decision carries the same risk, so not every decision is treated the same way. The split follows a simple logic: actions that stop money leaving your account happen automatically; actions that cause more money to leave it need you.

DecisionHandlingWhy
Stop an ad Automatic The worst case is a small amount of forgone opportunity on an ad that might have recovered. Waiting for approval costs more than the occasional wrong call.
Adjust an ad Automatic Launches at the same small controlled test budget as any new ad. The exposure is bounded and known in advance.
Increase budget Your approval The worst case here is real money spent on something that turns out not to work. That deserves a person, at least until the system has a track record you trust.

Approvals arrive as a short summary: what the system wants to do, what it observed, how confident it is, and what the budget would move from and to. Approving takes one click.

Approvals expire

A pending approval is only valid for a set window — two days by default. After that it lapses rather than sitting in a queue. Approving a four-day-old recommendation would mean acting on evidence the system has since moved past, so instead it re-examines the ad from scratch and proposes again if the case still holds.

Turning approval off

Once you are comfortable with how the system is behaving, budget increases can be moved to automatic on a per-client basis. Nothing about the underlying checks changes — every limit still applies exactly as before. The only difference is whether a person confirms the step. This is deliberately a switch you throw when you’ve seen enough, rather than the starting position.

The master switch

There is also a single global control that halts all automated activity immediately. When it is off, the system continues to gather data and form opinions, and continues to write them down — but it makes no changes to your account whatsoever. This is useful during busy trading periods, or any time you simply want everything to hold still.

08

The written record

Every autonomous action is written down before it happens, not after. If the record cannot be written, the action does not take place.

That ordering is deliberate and it is unusual. Most systems act first and log afterwards, which means any failure between the two leaves you with something that happened and no record of why. Reversing it guarantees the opposite: you may occasionally find a record of an action that was interrupted before it completed, but you will never find a change to your account that nobody can account for.

What each record contains

ElementPurpose
The decisionWhat was done, to which ad, and when.
The evidenceThe exact figures the decision was based on — spend, sales, cost per sale, click rates — frozen as they stood at that moment.
The confidenceHow certain the system was, and against what threshold. Recorded for every decision, including ones where it decided to do nothing.
The limits checkedEvery safety limit tested, with the numbers behind each, and whether it passed.
The reasoningA plain-language explanation of why this followed from that evidence.
The rules in forceWhich version of your limits applied at the time, so a past decision can be understood in its own context rather than today’s.

Why the historical figures are frozen

Performance data keeps changing after the fact, as described earlier. If the record simply pointed at “the numbers for that day”, then looking back a month later would show different figures than the ones the decision was actually made on — and the reasoning would look wrong even when it had been perfectly sound. Instead, each record keeps its own snapshot. You can always reconstruct not just what the system did, but what it knew when it did it.

A client should be able to see why an ad was stopped without anyone having to go digging through logs to reconstruct it.

In practice this means the dashboard can show, for any ad, a straightforward account: what it was testing, what it cost, how it compared, what was decided, and why. No technical interpretation required.

09

How it’s built

The system is arranged in layers, each with one job. The arrangement matters for a practical reason: it lets the same decision-making run across different ad platforms without being rewritten, and lets any one piece be replaced without disturbing the rest.

Why the translator layer exists

Every ad platform has its own vocabulary, its own limits on how often you may talk to it, and its own habits. All of that is contained inside the adapter for that platform. Above it, everything works in neutral terms — an ad, a budget, a sale.

The commercial benefit is straightforward: adding a new ad platform means writing one new translator, not rebuilding the system. The judgement, the safety limits, and the record keeping are all unchanged, so a second platform inherits the same standards as the first on day one rather than needing to earn them again.

Security of your account access

The system connects to your ad accounts through the platforms’ official authorisation process — the same consent screen you would see connecting any approved tool. It never asks for and never stores your password. The access credentials it does hold are encrypted at rest, are never displayed anywhere in the interface, and never appear in reports or diagnostic records. Access can be withdrawn by you at any time from within the ad platform itself, without our involvement.

10

When something goes wrong

Ad platforms go down, time out, and rate-limit. Any system that talks to them constantly will hit failures. What matters is what it does next.

Nothing happens twice

The most dangerous failure in a system like this is not an error — it is an uncertain error. A budget increase is sent, the connection drops, and no reply comes back. Did it take effect or not? A system that simply tries again risks applying the same increase twice.

Every instruction sent to an ad platform therefore carries a unique reference, and every budget instruction is expressed as a destination rather than a movement — “set this to £36”, never “add £6”. Applying that same instruction twice produces exactly the same result as applying it once. Before any retry, the system also checks whether the earlier attempt actually landed.

It notices when someone else made a change

If a person adjusts a budget directly in the ad platform after the system formed its plan, the system detects the discrepancy and abandons its action rather than overwriting the change. It then re-examines the ad with current information. Your manual changes are never silently undone.

Repeated failures stop the system trying

When a platform fails persistently, the system stops attempting rather than hammering it — which would risk the account being rate-limited or suspended. Attempts resume automatically once the platform recovers.

Nothing is dropped silently

Any action that cannot be completed after reasonable retries is set aside in a queue that a person reviews. It is never quietly abandoned. The one outcome the system will not produce is something that was supposed to happen, didn’t, and nobody found out.

Incomplete information means no decision

If performance data cannot be collected for some ads in a test group, the system does not proceed using the ads it did manage to read. Comparing the ones that happened to report against a partial picture of the rest produces a confident answer drawn from a distorted comparison — which is worse than no answer. The whole group is skipped until the picture is complete again.

11

What you’ll see

The dashboard is built around one question: what is happening to my money, and why? Everything else is secondary to answering that clearly.

01
Live position

What is running now, what each ad is costing, and how the whole account is performing against your targets. Ads move visibly between stages — testing, scaling, stopped — so a change of status is something you notice rather than something you have to go looking for.

02
Decision history

Every action taken, in plain language, with the figures behind it. Written to be read by you, not interpreted by a specialist.

03
Approvals

Anything waiting on you, with the reasoning attached and the time remaining before it lapses.

04
Creative library

Every ad produced, grouped by the idea it was testing, with results attached — so you can see which arguments are working on your audience, not just which individual ads did well.

05
Limits and settings

Your targets, caps and thresholds, adjustable at any time. Changes take effect on the next cycle, and past decisions remain interpretable against the limits that applied when they were made.

On accessibility

The interface works on a phone as well as a desktop, is fully navigable by keyboard, and respects the system setting that reduces motion for people who find animation uncomfortable. Movement in the interface is used to show something changing — an ad moving from testing to scaling — rather than for decoration.

Glossary

Ad variant
One individual ad — a specific headline, body text, call to action and image.
Angle
The underlying argument an ad is making, as distinct from its exact wording. “Saves you time” is an angle; a particular sentence about saving time is a variant of it.
Attribution settling
The delay between someone acting on an ad and the ad platform crediting that action to the right day. Reported figures for recent days keep rising for roughly three days afterwards.
Confidence
How likely it is that an observed difference reflects something real rather than chance. Expressed as a percentage; the system requires a high figure before spending more money and a somewhat lower one before stopping an ad.
Cost per acquisition (CPA)
What you pay, on average, for one sale or lead. Total spend divided by the number of conversions.
Return on ad spend (ROAS)
Revenue produced for each unit of currency spent. A ROAS of 3 means £3 back for every £1 spent.
Conversion
The action you are paying for — a purchase, an enquiry, a signup. Defined by you when the account is configured.
Guardrail
A hard limit enforced by the software itself. Distinct from a setting or a preference: a guardrail cannot be exceeded, only changed by you.
Test group
A set of ads launched together and compared against one another. Grouping them makes the comparison fair, because they run at the same time under the same conditions.
Statistical significance
A formal test of whether a difference between two things is large enough, given how much data there is, to be treated as real rather than coincidence.
